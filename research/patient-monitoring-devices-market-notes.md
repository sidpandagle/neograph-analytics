# Patient monitoring devices — research notes
**Slug:** patient-monitoring-devices-market   **Drafted:** 2026-10-18

Scope drafted here: **hospital and acute-care multiparameter patient monitoring
equipment** — bedside multiparameter monitors across high, mid and low acuity,
transport and telemetry monitors, central stations and monitoring networks, the
measurement modules and clinical software that sit on them, and the manufacturer
service and consumable-accessory revenue attached to the installed base. It is
the monitor at the bed, the central station at the nursing desk and the telemetry
network between them.

**Not** remote patient monitoring, **not** consumer or clinical wearables,
**not** self-monitoring or continuous glucose monitoring, **not** standalone
spot-check pulse oximetry sold into the home, and **not** the clinical service
revenue of monitoring a patient. The corpus already carries
`remote-patient-monitoring-market` and `wearable-medical-devices-market` as
separate published rows [29][30], and `continuous-glucose-monitoring-market`
besides. Section "Resolving the two scope traps" below quantifies the boundary
rather than asserting it.

## The spread

**Raw, across all fourteen global estimates on a 2026 base: $9.04B to $186.07B —
a 20.58x spread.** The CAGRs run **5.655% to 22.9%, a 4.05x spread.**

**After excluding Verified Market Research** for failing its own arithmetic
(above), thirteen estimates span **$9.04B to $63.61B — a 7.04x spread**, and
their growth rates span **5.655% to 10.462%, a 1.85x spread**.

```
7.037 / 1.850 = 3.80
```

**The sizes disagree 3.80x more than the growth rates do.** That is the opposite
of the radiotherapy equipment result in this corpus, and the reason is exactly
the one the brief anticipated: in radiotherapy there is one thing and a countable
fleet of it, so publishers argue about the replacement cycle. In patient
monitoring there is no agreement about what the product *is*, and a 7x
disagreement about size with a 1.85x disagreement about growth is the signature
of a definition problem, not a forecasting problem.

### The spread is three tiers, and the tier boundaries are nameable

Sort the thirteen surviving estimates by what their own segmentation includes,
and the 7.04x spread collapses:

| Tier | What it includes | 2026 range | Spread | CAGR range | Spread |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Hospital / bedside multiparameter** — our scope | Bedside, transport and telemetry monitors, central stations, acuity tiers. **No wearable or patch device type, no glucose.** [1][3][7][5] | $9.04B - $14.29B | **1.58x** | 5.655% - 9.320% | 1.65x |
| **2. Multiparameter with wearable or remote device types added** | Tier 1 plus a wearable/remote monitor device type or a home-and-remote end-user segment [4][6][2] | $13.20B - $21.07B | **1.60x** | 7.272% - 9.150% | 1.26x |
| **3. Broad "patient monitoring"** | All monitoring modalities; four of six include blood glucose monitoring; one adds RPM hubs, patch monitors and monitoring *services* [11][8][12][10][9][13] | $55.40B - $63.61B | **1.15x** | 7.042% - 10.462% | 1.49x |

**Within every tier the publishers agree to within 1.6x. Across the tiers they
differ by 7x.** The $9.04B-to-$63.61B spread is therefore not uncertainty about
the market; it is three different markets wearing the same name. Tier 3 is a
7.04x restatement of tier 1 because it has added self-monitoring blood glucose,
consumer and clinical wearables, remote monitoring hubs and, in Mordor's case,
telehealth and managed-monitoring services.

In tier 1 the size spread (1.58x) and the CAGR spread (1.65x) are essentially
equal — `1.581 / 1.648 = 0.96`. Once the definition is fixed, the remaining
disagreement is evenly split between level and slope, which is what a
well-behaved market looks like.

## Resolving the two scope traps

### Trap 1 — this report is not additive with the remote and wearable rows

The corpus already carries two published rows that a reader could be tempted to
add to this one [29][30]. Normalised to a 2026 base at their own stated rates:

```
remote-patient-monitoring-market    $26.0B (2023) x 1.199^3 = $44.82B
wearable-medical-devices-market     $55.7B (2023) x 1.16^3  = $86.94B
                                                      sum    = $131.76B
```

Our $9.04B is **6.9%** of those two combined. The question is not whether it is
small; it is how much of it is already inside them.

**Bound 1 — the same publisher, the same window, with and without wearables.**
This is the cleanest measurement in this file, because it holds publisher,
method and base year constant and varies only the scope:

```
360iResearch, bedside multiparameter, no wearable device type [1]:   $9.04B (2026)
360iResearch, multiparameter systems incl. Wearable portability
  and Home Care end user [2]:                                       $21.07B (2026)

$21.07B - $9.04B = $12.03B
$9.04B / $21.07B = 42.9%
$21.07B / $9.04B = 2.331x
```

**Adding wearable and home-care monitoring to a bedside scope multiplies it by
2.33x.** Our scope is the 42.9%. The $12.03B difference is the part that belongs
to the remote and wearable rows, and this report does not count it.

**Bound 2 — the ceiling on what could double-count.** The only named end-user
split in our evidence base for a multiparameter scope is Mordor's: **hospitals
and ICUs 67.94% of 2025 revenue** [4]. Applying its complement as a ceiling:

```
$9,040M x (1 - 0.6794) = $2,898M at most outside hospitals and ICUs
$2,898M / $44,820M  remote patient monitoring (2026-equivalent) = 6.47%
$2,898M / $131,758M both rows combined                          = 2.20%
```

So **at most 2.20% of the remote-plus-wearable total could be double-counted by
publishing this report, and the true figure is lower**, because that $2,898M
ceiling includes ambulatory surgery centres and specialty clinics — facility
settings, not remote monitoring. Two caveats are stated rather than buried: the
67.94% is Mordor's split of its own scope, which *includes* a wearable/remote
device type, applied to 360iResearch's bedside figure, which does not; and the
two corpus rows have a 2023 base, so their 2026-equivalents are our arithmetic on
their own stated CAGRs, not their publishers' 2026 estimates.

**Bound 3 — the U.S. market, from a publisher that sizes both at once.** iData
Research's U.S. patient monitoring equipment market has Multi-Parameter Vital
Signs Monitoring as segment 1 and **Remote Patient Monitoring as segment 3 of
sixteen** [15], at "over $10 billion in 2025". Against the U.S. slice of our
scope (see division 1 for the share bracket and its sources):

```
$9,040M x 0.360  = $3,254M -> 32.5% of iData's $10,000M
$9,040M x 0.4185 = $3,783M -> 37.8% of iData's $10,000M
```

Bedside multiparameter monitoring is roughly **a third of all U.S. monitoring
equipment spend**, and the other two-thirds — remote monitoring, pulse oximetry,
ECG, blood pressure, temperature management, EEG, IONM and the rest — is outside
this report by construction.

### Trap 2 — which published estimates silently include remote monitoring

Nine of the fifteen do, and each is placed in a named tier rather than in the
spread. The evidence is on the publishers' own pages:

- **Mordor patient monitoring [13]** — the most explicit: its covered-segment
  list names "wearable and patch-based monitors, remote patient monitoring (RPM)
  hubs and gateways" and, separately, "RPM and telehealth services" and "managed
  monitoring and triage". It is the largest coherent estimate in the set at
  $63.61B, and that is why. Tier 3.
- **Mordor multiparameter [4]** — "Wearable/Remote Monitors" is a device-type
  segment. Tier 2.
- **360iResearch systems [2]** — "Wearable" is a portability segment and "Home
  Care" an end user. Tier 2.
- **Next Move Strategy Consulting [6]** — "Home & Remote Care Settings" is an
  end-user segment. Tier 2.
- **Global Market Insights [8]** — "smart wearable ECG patches" is a cardiac
  product. Tier 3.
- **Grand View [9]** — wearable ECG, wearable continuous temperature monitoring
  and wearable drug delivery systems are product sub-segments. Tier 3.
- **Precedence [12]**, **Fortune [10]** — homecare end users plus blood glucose
  monitoring. Tier 3.
- **Coherent [11]** — Home Healthcare end user; no glucose. Tier 3.
- **iData [15]** — Remote Patient Monitoring is segment 3 of 16. Used only as
  the U.S. denominator in bound 3, never in the spread.

**The four in tier 1 are the ones whose published segmentation contains no
wearable, patch or remote device type**: 360iResearch bedside [1], The Business
Research Company [3], Research Nester [7] and Polaris [5]. Homecare appears as an
end-user setting in [7] and [5] — a bedside-class monitor used at home — which is
the residual the 2.20% ceiling above is built to bound.

### A third trap the register exposes: the FDA code does not draw the line either

The pipeline row names "FDA 510(k) clearances under the physiological-monitor
product codes". Those codes are **MWI** — monitor, physiological, patient
(without arrhythmia detection or alarms), 21 CFR 870.2300 — and **MHX** — with
arrhythmia detection or alarms, 21 CFR 870.1025 [20]. Counted on the openFDA
510(k) endpoint, data last updated 5 October 2026 [19]:

```
MHX  430 clearances all-time (earliest 1987)
MWI  308 clearances all-time (earliest 1999)
     738 clearances across the two codes
```

Distinct applicant strings all-time: 115 under MHX, 137 under MWI. In the recent
window **1 January 2021 to 31 December 2026: 95 clearances from 46 distinct
applicant strings** (54 clearances / 16 applicants under MHX; 41 / 34 under MWI).
Collapsing obvious corporate-name variants — Analog Devices x2, Capsule x2, Nihon
Kohden x2, Philips Böblingen x2, Shenzhen Mindray x2, Draeger plus Shanghai
Draeger — leaves roughly **40 distinct active firms**.

And here is the trap, countable: **11 of those 46 applicant strings (23.9%) are
remote, home or wearable monitoring companies, not hospital bedside monitor
makers** — AliveCor, Casana Care, Cosinuss, Empatica, Happy Health, iHealth Labs,
Medopad, Medwand Solutions, Multisensor Diagnostics (Aidar Health), AMC Health
and Ward 24/7 ApS. Empatica's EmbracePlus wrist wearable cleared as K242737 on 6
June 2025 and Cosinuss's c-med0 alpha in-ear sensor as K253436 on 30 April 2026
— both under MWI, the same product code as a Philips IntelliVue or a Dräger
Infinity bedside monitor [28].

**The regulatory classification is therefore useless as a market boundary.** A
wrist-worn consumer-form sensor and an ICU bedside monitor share a product code.
Anyone who builds a "patient monitoring" market by counting cleared devices under
these codes will produce tier 3, not tier 1, and will not know they have done it.
The boundary has to be drawn on what the device is for and where it sits, which
is what the three disclosed vendor segments in division 3 let us do.

## Grounding arithmetic

Three registers and three disclosed vendor segments. The FX basis first, then
five divisions.

### FX basis

Philips reports in euro and its fiscal year is the calendar year, so the ECB
**2025 annual average** reference rate is an exact match rather than an
approximation. From the Bank of Finland's publication of the ECB daily fixings
[27]: **EUR 1 = USD 1.1300**. GE HealthCare and Baxter report in US dollars; no
conversion is applied to either.

### The registers

**AHA Annual Survey, via Fast Facts on U.S. Hospitals, 2026 edition, drawn from
the 2024 AHA Annual Survey** [16], quoting the page's own labels:

```
Total Number of All U.S. Hospitals        6,100
Number of U.S. Community Hospitals        5,121
Total Staffed Beds in All U.S. Hospitals  907,216
Staffed Beds in Community Hospitals       775,297
Total Admissions in All U.S. Hospitals    35,658,583
```

**CMS Provider of Services file, as analysed by MedPAC in its July 2026 Data
Book, Section 6** [17] — the cross-check the pipeline row asks for:

> "In FY 2024, there were approximately 4,400 general acute care hospitals that
> provided at least one inpatient stay."

MedPAC's note is important and is carried forward: *"General acute care
hospitals" refers to Subsection (d) hospitals, critical access hospitals, and
short-term acute care hospitals in territories… "Number of hospitals" is the
number of Medicare provider numbers, rounded to the nearest 10; a provider number
can represent multiple hospital locations.* Of those ~4,400, about two-thirds are
paid under the IPPS, about 30% are critical access hospitals and about 2% under
other methodologies.

```
AHA 6,100 hospitals / CMS PoS 4,400 general acute care = 1.386x
907,216 staffed beds / 6,100 hospitals = 149 beds per hospital
907,216 staffed beds / 4,400 acute hospitals = 206 beds per acute hospital
```

**The two registers disagree by 1.39x, and the gap is the market.** AHA's 6,100
includes psychiatric, rehabilitation, long-term care, specialty and federal
hospitals; CMS's 4,400 is general acute care only. Monitoring capital concentrates
in the 4,400, and in the two-thirds of those that are IPPS hospitals rather than
the 30% that are critical access hospitals — which have 25 or fewer inpatient
beds and ran at a **38% occupancy rate in FY2024** [17]. A bed that is empty
three-fifths of the time is still a staffed bed in the denominator; it is not a
monitored bed.

**SCCM Critical Care Statistics, on the AHA 2023 Annual Survey** [18]: **112,369
ICU beds** across more than 5,000 ICUs in **5,222 hospitals** — those with at
least 25 acute care beds and at least one ICU bed, a subset of 6,120 acute care
registered hospitals — representing **12.2% of the 916,752 staffed beds** at
those hospitals. SCCM also states plainly that *"AHA offers no data on bed
utilization."*

### 1. Dollars per staffed bed — the division the register is for

Our published figure is global; the bed register is American. So the U.S. share
has to come from a named source, and three give one for a monitoring scope:
**Fortune Business Insights puts the U.S. at "roughly 36.0% of global sales" in
2026** [10]; **Mordor puts North America at 41.85% of the multiparameter market
in 2025** [4]; **Coherent puts North America at 42.1% in 2026** [11]. The U.S.
bracket used is **36.0% to 41.85%** — the first is U.S.-specific, the second is
the North American figure for the closest scope, and the gap between them is the
honest width of the apportionment.

```
$9,040M x 0.3600  = $3,254.4M U.S.
$9,040M x 0.4185  = $3,783.2M U.S.

$3,254.4M / 907,216 staffed beds = $3,587 per staffed bed per year
$3,783.2M / 907,216 staffed beds = $4,170 per staffed bed per year
```

Against community-hospital beds only, which is the closer denominator because
federal and long-term-care beds are not in the addressable base:

```
$3,254.4M / 775,297 = $4,198 per community staffed bed per year
$3,783.2M / 775,297 = $4,880 per community staffed bed per year
```

**Roughly $3,600 to $4,900 of bedside multiparameter monitoring equipment,
software and attached service per U.S. staffed bed per year.** That is the
central arithmetic of this report, and the number worth quoting.

**What it rules out, immediately.** Push the same numerator onto ICU beds alone:

```
$3,254.4M / 112,369 ICU beds = $28,961 per ICU bed per year
$3,783.2M / 112,369 ICU beds = $33,668 per ICU bed per year
```

**$29,000-33,700 per ICU bed per year is not a credible figure for high-acuity
monitoring**, even including central stations, network, modules and service — it
would amount to replacing every ICU monitor roughly annually. So the market
cannot be an ICU market. It is spread across the ICU beds *and* the 87.8% of
staffed beds that are not ICU beds [18], which is exactly what the publisher's
own device-type segmentation says: high-, mid- **and low-acuity** monitors [1].
This division rules in the whole-hospital reading and rules out the
critical-care-only reading, using two registers and no price assumption.

### 2. The same division against the broad tier — why tier 3 cannot be this market

Run division 1 on Mordor's $63.61B broad figure [13] instead, at its own stated
North America share of 41.2%:

```
$63,610M x 0.412 = $26,207M North America
$26,207M / 907,216 staffed beds = $28,888 per staffed bed per year
```

**$28,888 per staffed bed per year**, or 7.0x the tier 1 figure. Four U.S.
hospital beds would between them consume $115,000 a year of monitoring. No
hospital capital budget looks like that, and the reason is that the numerator is
not hospital spend: it contains self-monitoring blood glucose strips bought by
patients, consumer wearables, remote monitoring subscriptions and telehealth
services, none of which is attached to a staffed bed at all. iData's U.S. figure
makes the same point from the other direction [15]:

```
$10,000M / 907,216 staffed beds = $11,023 per staffed bed per year
                                  (all 16 U.S. monitoring segments, incl. RPM)
```

Even the U.S. all-monitoring figure is $11,023 per staffed bed, well under half
the $28,888 the broad global tier implies per bed. The broad tier is describing
a population, not a bed count.

**The decisive comparison.** Philips, GE HealthCare and Baxter are, by common
consent and by the 510(k) register, three of the handful of firms that actually
supply hospital bedside monitoring. At the tier 1 level they account for 84.4% of
the market, which is tight but is what a concentrated capital-equipment market
looks like — and two things loosen it in the honest direction. GE's PCS segment
is **not** a monitoring segment: its FY2025 Form 10-K describes it as Monitoring
Solutions (Patient Monitoring, Diagnostic Cardiology) **plus Life Support
Solutions (Maternal Infant Care, Anesthesia)**, with services, consumables and
digital applications across both [24]. Baxter's Front Line Care likewise spans
Welch Allyn physical-assessment instruments and diagnostic cardiology alongside
vital-signs monitors, and Baxter's FY2025 commentary attributes the segment's 2%
growth *"primarily driven by growth in our cardiology products partially offset
by a strategic product exit within our respiratory health products business"*
[25]. And the Philips line carries Emergency Care. Strip all three of what is not
bedside monitoring and the ratio falls well below 84.4%.

**At the tier 3 level the same three vendors account for 12.0-13.7%.** Three of
the four or five firms that make hospital patient monitors cannot be an eighth of
the patient monitoring market. That single ratio is what rules tier 3 out as a
description of hospital monitoring, independently of every scope statement
quoted above. And at Verified Market Research's $186.07B they would be **4.1%**,
which is not a market structure that exists anywhere in medical devices.

### 4. The vendor's own share claim, backed out — and why it is not published

Philips' Capital Markets Day states **"1 out of every 2 patient monitors,
globally"** as its installed-base position and a **"~54% market share"** for
Patient Monitors, footnoted as *"Philips estimated equipment market share based
on the last twelve months for Patient Monitors; data on file"* [22]. That is a
vendor self-estimate on undisclosed data. It is **not** published as a market
share anywhere in the JSON. But it can be divided, and the result is a check:

```
$3,384.2M / 0.54 = $6,267M implied patient-monitor equipment market
$6,267M / $9,040M = 69.3%

2 x $3,384.2M    = $6,768M implied, on the installed-base claim instead
$6,768M / $9,040M = 74.9%
```

**Two back-outs from the same vendor's two different claims land at $6.27B and
$6.77B — 69% and 75% of the $9.04B we publish.** They should land *below* it,
and they do, for a reason that is visible rather than assumed: Philips' 54% is an
*equipment* share for *Patient Monitors*, while the $9.04B scope also contains
central stations, telemetry networks, monitoring software, modules and
manufacturer service, much of which Philips' denominator excludes. Two vendor
claims, converted at a stated FX rate, reconstruct 69-75% of an independent
publisher's figure. That is corroboration of magnitude, and it is as far as a
self-estimated share can be pushed.

### 5. The replacement arithmetic, and what is deliberately not computed

The obvious next division — monitors per bed times price per monitor divided by
replacement cycle — is **not performed**, because no source in this file gives a
monitor price or a replacement cycle. What can be said from the registers alone:

```
907,216 staffed beds [16]
112,369 of them ICU beds, 12.26% [18]
$3,587-4,170 per staffed bed per year (division 1)
```

On a seven-year equipment cycle that is $25,100-29,200 of monitoring capital,
software and service per staffed bed across the cycle; on a ten-year cycle,
$35,900-41,700. Both figures include attached service and accessory revenue, and
both are spread over *all* staffed beds including the unmonitored ones, so
neither is a monitor price and neither is presented as one. The cycle lengths are
illustrative arithmetic on a sourced annual figure, not sourced cycle lengths,
and nothing in the JSON depends on them.

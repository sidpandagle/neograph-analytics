# Clinical Laboratory Services — research notes
**Slug:** clinical-laboratory-services-market   **Drafted:** 2026-10-18

Pipeline row 54, round 7A. Industry: Life Sciences.

This market has an unusually good public register behind it, and an unusually bad
published literature in front of it. The certificate register is a federal
census; the unit price of the commonest tests is set by regulation and published
annually; the Medicare program total is audited by the HHS Office of Inspector
General under a statutory reporting requirement; and the two largest independent
operators are SEC registrants who disclose an **absolute requisition count**,
which is the single most useful denominator in diagnostics and almost never
available. Against that, one published "United States clinical laboratory
services market" is smaller than one of those two companies.

## Where this report's boundary sits

Three slugs in this corpus touch the same CLIA register and must not share a
denominator. Stated explicitly, because reusing one would be the easiest
mistake to make here:

| Report | What it counts | CLIA denominator it belongs on |
|---|---|---|
| `point-of-care-diagnostics-market` (published) | Products and consumables used for near-patient testing, including self-monitoring of blood glucose | **Certificate of Waiver (233,448) + Provider-Performed Microscopy (21,736) = 255,184** sites whose certificate permits only point-of-care-grade testing |
| `liquid-biopsy-market` (published) | A single assay class — circulating tumour DNA and related blood-based oncology tests | No site register; an assay-class market, a subset of the genetic-testing line inside *this* report's boundary |
| **`clinical-laboratory-services-market` (this report)** | **Revenue earned by laboratories for performing testing on patient specimens** — the service, not the instrument, the reagent or the kit | **Certificate of Compliance (15,214) + Certificate of Accreditation (15,542) = 30,756** US laboratories whose certificate permits moderate- and/or high-complexity testing |

A Certificate of Waiver permits a site to perform *only* waived tests. Those
255,184 sites are the point-of-care report's denominator and are **not** this
report's: a physician office running four waived tests a day is not a clinical
laboratory service provider in the sense any of the houses below are sizing.
The 30,756 Compliance-plus-Accreditation set is the register of laboratories
permitted to run the moderate- and high-complexity work — hospital laboratories,
independent and reference laboratories, large pathology practices — which is
what every published "clinical laboratory services" scope describes.

Both figures come from the same CMS table (source 1). Where this report divides
by 30,756 and the point-of-care report divides by 255,184, the two are
different rows of one register applied to different numerators, and neither
numerator is reused.

**The other boundary, and the one the literature blurs:** clinical laboratory
*services* is the laboratory's revenue for performing a test. In-vitro
diagnostics (IVD) is the sale of the analyser, the reagent and the kit to that
laboratory. They are different sides of one transaction and must not be added.
Exactly one house in the table below publishes a scope statement that excludes
instruments and reagents by name. That fact decides which series this report
publishes.

## The spread

**At the base year, the houses nearly agree. On growth, they do not.** That is
the reverse of the usual pattern in this corpus, and it is the finding.

- **2026 global base values, eight houses: 215.88 bn (360iResearch) to 319.49 bn
  (Coherent) — a 1.480x spread.** Narrow by the standards of this corpus; the
  oncology-information-systems set spread 2.74x and point-of-care 11.0x.
- **Published CAGRs, nine houses: 3.5% (Precision Business Insights) to 6.9%
  (Coherent) — a 1.97x spread.** Wider than the base-year spread.
- **Scope does explain part of the base-year gap, in the expected direction.**
  The two highest global series are the two whose scopes reach furthest: Coherent
  at 319.49 bn and Data Bridge at 319.13 bn, the latter explicitly including drug
  discovery, toxicology, cell and gene therapy and preclinical and clinical trial
  services, which are contract-research revenue and not specimen testing. The
  lowest, 360iResearch at 215.88 bn, is the one that segments by instrument
  technology, suggesting a scope assembled from the IVD side of the transaction.
- **The outlier is not a scope difference, it is an error.** MarkNtel's
  United-States-only 9.89 bn for 2025 is ruled out absolutely in grounding (d)
  by a single SEC filing.

**Why growth is the contested variable here, and not scope.** In most markets in
this corpus the houses agree on a growth rate and disagree on the boundary.
Here they roughly agree on the boundary and disagree by nearly two times on
growth, because growth in clinical laboratory services is a **price-regulation
question, not a volume question.** Volume is slow, demographic and well
measured; price in the largest single market is set by statute, has already been
cut once, and is scheduled to be cut again. A house that models the Protecting
Access to Medicare Act cuts landing gets 3.5%; a house that models them being
deferred again gets 6.9%. Both are forecasting Congress, not laboratories.
The published literature does not say which it is doing.

## Grounding arithmetic

### (a) Per certified laboratory — and what it rules out for each denominator

The register, from CMS's Division of Clinical Laboratory Improvement and
Quality, September 2026 (source 1):

| CLIA category, September 2026 | Laboratories |
|---|---|
| Registered (exempt and non-exempt) | 301,284 |
| Registered (non-exempt only) | 285,940 |
| Certificate of Waiver | 233,448 |
| Provider-Performed Microscopy | 21,736 |
| **Certificate of Compliance** | **15,214** |
| **Certificate of Accreditation** | **15,542** |
| of which COLA 5,639, College of American Pathologists 6,966, The Joint Commission 2,336 | — |
| Exempt states: New York 7,235, Washington 8,106 | — |

```
15,214 (Compliance) + 15,542 (Accreditation) = 30,756 US laboratories whose
                                               certificate permits moderate-
                                               and/or high-complexity testing
```

Fortune Business Insights is the only house publishing a **United States dollar
figure for 2026** — 103.79 bn — so it is the only numerator that can be divided
by a US denominator without mismatching the two. Dividing it three ways:

```
$103,790,000,000 / 30,756  certified moderate/high-complexity labs = $3,374,626 per lab per year
$103,790,000,000 / 255,184 waived + PPM sites                      =   $406,726 per site per year
$103,790,000,000 / 301,284 all registered labs                     =   $344,492 per lab per year
```

**What it rules in.** $3,374,626 a year — about $3.37 m — is a coherent average
for the 30,756 set. That set is hospital laboratories serving inpatient and
outreach work, independent and reference laboratories, and large pathology
practices. A $3.37 m annual book is a mid-sized hospital laboratory. The
distribution behind that mean is extremely skewed, which is why it is a
plausibility test and not an estimate.

**What it rules out.** $406,726 a year at a site whose certificate permits
**only waived testing** is about $1,114 of testing every day of the year at a
physician office that may run a handful of strep A, urinalysis, glucose and
HbA1c tests. The waived-and-PPM denominator therefore cannot carry a clinical
laboratory *services* numerator, which is precisely why it belongs to the
point-of-care report and not to this one. The two reports divide different
numerators by different rows of the same register, deliberately.

The same division on the global headline published below is a **global numerator
over a US denominator** and is therefore an upper bound only:

```
$315,140,000,000 (Mordor, global, 2026) / 30,756 US certified labs = $10,246,456 per lab per year
```

The true global per-laboratory figure is lower than $10,246,456, because the
rest of the world adds laboratories without being counted in the CLIA register.
Stated here as a bound, never as a measurement.

### (b) Per requisition, from audited filings, against the statutory price

This is the strongest grounding available in diagnostics and it exists only
because one registrant publishes an absolute volume.

Quest Diagnostics, Form 10-K for the year ended 31 December 2025 (source 5):
**"Processed approximately 244 million test requisitions in 2025"**, with
diagnostic information services revenue of **$10,785 million** for the year, up
12.2%. Of those requisitions, **approximately 23 million** were performed by
LifeLabs in Canada.

```
$10,785,000,000 / 244,000,000 requisitions = $44.20 of revenue per requisition
```

Now the published statutory price of the commonest routine work. Medicare
Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule amounts carried on 2026 schedules (source 9):

| CPT | Test | 2026 amount |
|---|---|---|
| 80053 | Comprehensive metabolic panel | $10.56 |
| 85025 | Complete blood count with automated differential | $7.77 |
| 80061 | Lipid panel | $13.39 |
| **Sum of the three commonest routine panels** | | **$31.72** |

```
$44.20 per requisition / $31.72 (three routine panels at Medicare rates) = 1.393
```

**What it rules in.** A blended all-payer $44.20 per requisition sitting at
1.393x the Medicare price of three routine panels is exactly what the structure
predicts: a requisition carries more than one test, commercial payers pay above
the fee schedule, and the mix includes esoteric and genetic work priced far
above a metabolic panel. Quest's own filing says the number of tests per
requisition rose in 2025 and that revenue per requisition rose 0.1% overall but
**2.4% organically**, the gap being LifeLabs, "which has a lower revenue per
requisition". Had this division produced $4 or $400, one of the two series would
be wrong.

Labcorp's FY2025 10-K (source 4) does not publish an absolute requisition count.
Its Diagnostics segment revenue was **$10,876.5 million**, up 7.2% from
$10,144.3 million, on requisition volume up 3.7% (organic 2.2%, acquisitions net
of divestitures 1.5%) and price/mix up 3.5%. The company states it "performed
more than 750 million tests" in 2025 across approximately 100 countries with
nearly 71,000 employees, and that it "supports approximately 175 million patient
encounters annually":

```
$10,876,500,000 / 175,000,000 patient encounters = $62.15 per encounter
$10,876,500,000 / 750,000,000 tests             = $14.50 per test
```

Both are **deliberately mismatched ratios**, stated as bounds: the encounter and
test counts are company-wide while the revenue is one segment, so the true
per-encounter and per-test figures are lower. $14.50 per test against a $7.77
Medicare price for a CBC and a $10.56 price for a metabolic panel brackets
correctly, which is the only thing it is offered as.

### (c) The share of United States health spending — where the growth argument is settled

The one grounding in this market that is independent of every market-research
house. It uses a peer-reviewed share and the federal national accounts.

Rohr et al., *PLoS ONE* 2016 (source 10), define in-vitro diagnostic spending as
"payments to clinical laboratories for testing services" and report it rising
"from US$30 billion in 1998 to an estimated US$67 billion in 2013", which
"represents approximately 2.3% of all healthcare spending in the US".

**First, validate the share against the national accounts rather than trusting
it.** CMS's Office of the Actuary reported total US national health expenditure
for 2013 at $2.9 trillion, $9,255 per person, 17.4% of GDP (source 11):

```
$67,000,000,000 / $2,900,000,000,000 = 2.3103%
```

The published share and the published dollars agree to 0.01 points. The PLOS
figure is internally consistent with the federal total, so it can be used.

**Then hold the share and advance it on the national accounts.** CMS's Office of
the Actuary projects 2026 national health spending at **$6,017.4 bn**, 18.7% of
GDP, against an actual **$5,278.6 bn** for 2024 at $15,474 per person (sources 11,
12, 13 — $5,278.6 bn over $15,474 implies a population of 341.13 million, which
is the two CMS figures agreeing with each other):

```
0.0231 x $6,017.4 bn = $139.00 bn  US payments to clinical laboratories, 2026,
                                   IF the 2013 share of health spending held
```

Against Fortune Business Insights' published US figure of $103.79 bn for 2026:

```
$103.79 bn / $6,017.4 bn = 1.7248%  of projected 2026 US health spending
(2.3103% - 1.7248%) / 2.3103% = 25.3% relative decline from the 2013 share
$139.00 bn / $315.14 bn = 44.11%  the US share of the global market that a
                                  constant 2.31% share would force
```

**The two readings, stated as a fork rather than resolved by preference.** Either
the United States is **44.11%** of the global market — above every published
North America share in the table except Mordor's own 41.3%, and far above
Fortune Business Insights' own stated 33.8% — **or** payments to clinical
laboratories have fallen from 2.31% of US health spending in 2013 to about
**1.72%** in 2026, a quarter of the share gone in thirteen years.

**The second reading is the one the statute supports, and it is the growth
story.** The Protecting Access to Medicare Act of 2014 cut Clinical Laboratory
Fee Schedule rates across 2018-2020. Quest's own 10-K states that CMS set those
rates on laboratory-reported data in which "independent laboratories were
overrepresented, and hospitals and physician office laboratories were
underrepresented, making the first round of PAMA cuts excessive", and that "the
first three years of cuts greatly exceeded the original 10-year savings
projections". Approximately **800** laboratory services were scheduled for cuts
of up to **15%** in 2026; the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026, signed
**3 February 2026**, blocked them, moved the private-payer data reporting window
to **1 May - 31 July 2026** on January-June 2025 data, and left reductions of up
to 15% per year authorised for **2027, 2028 and 2029** (sources 14, 15, 16).

A market whose largest single market has had its regulated unit price cut once
and has three more years of 15% cuts authorised is not obviously a 6.9% market.
It is also not obviously a 3.5% market, because volume and test mix are both
rising. That is the honest state of the evidence, and it is why the CAGR spread
here is wider than the scope spread.

### (d) The rule-out: one company is larger than one published market

```
$10,876,500,000  Labcorp Diagnostics segment revenue, FY2025 (10-K, filed 2026-02-24)
/ $9,890,000,000  MarkNtel Advisors' entire US clinical laboratory services market, 2025
= 1.0997x
```

Quest's US diagnostic information services revenue is not separately disclosed,
but a floor follows from its own volume split. LifeLabs performed approximately
23 of the 244 million requisitions, and Quest states LifeLabs carries a *lower*
revenue per requisition, so the non-LifeLabs share of revenue exceeds
221/244:

```
$10,785 m x (1 - 23/244) = $9,768 m  floor on Quest's non-LifeLabs DIS revenue
$10,876.5 m + $9,768 m   = $20,645 m combined, the two largest US independents
$20,645 m / $9,890 m     = 2.087x    MarkNtel's entire US market
```

**Two companies are 2.087 times the whole market as MarkNtel sizes it.** That is
not a scope difference; there is no definition of "United States clinical
laboratory services" under which Labcorp and Quest are excluded. The series is
recorded in the table and never used.

The same two companies against the credible US figures:

```
$20,645 m / $103,790 m (FBI, US 2026)                  = 19.89%
$20,645 m / $139,000 m (PLOS share on 2026 accounts)   = 14.85%
$20,645 m / $73,060 m  (SNS Insider, US 2025)          = 28.26%
```

**19.89% and 14.85% are coherent; 28.26% is not.** Every house in the table that
publishes a service-provider split puts hospital-based laboratories at 49.7%
(Fortune Business Insights, 2026), 53.70% (Mordor, 2025) or 54.7% (Coherent,
2026) of the market. If the two largest independents alone were 28.26% of the
United States, then the independents as a class — which also include Sonic
Healthcare's US operations, BioReference, ARUP, Mayo Clinic Laboratories,
NeoGenomics, Natera, Exact Sciences, Guardant and Myriad, none of them small —
would have to exceed the hospital sector, contradicting all three of those
published splits. SNS Insider's $73.06 bn for the US is also only **1.282%** of
the $5,700 bn CMS projects for 2025 health spending, against the 2.31% measured
for 2013. It is recorded as the low bound and not used.

### (e) What the Medicare program actually spends, and where the growth is

The HHS Office of Inspector General reports Medicare Part B laboratory spending
annually under a statutory requirement. Report **OEI-09-25-00330**, issued
23 January 2026 (source 8): Part B spending on clinical laboratory tests was
**about $8.4 bn in 2024, up 5%** on 2023. Genetic tests were **43%** of it,
**over $3.6 bn**, on about **5%** of the tests performed, and genetic spending
rose **20%** in one year while non-genetic spending **fell to $4.8 bn** and has
generally declined since 2021. The top 25 procedure codes were **over $4.1 bn**,
almost half of the total, ten of them genetic. The highest-spending single code
was **87798**, at **$442.5 m**, up **51%**, at a median payment of **$447** per
claim. The number of Part B enrollees receiving laboratory tests **fell 15% from
2018 to 2024**.

```
$8.4 bn (Medicare Part B lab, 2024) / $103.79 bn (FBI, US 2026) = 8.09%
$447 (median payment, code 87798) / $10.56 (CPT 80053, metabolic panel) = 42.3x
```

**What this rules in and what it explains.** Medicare fee-for-service Part B is
roughly 8% of United States clinical laboratory payments — small, because it
covers a minority of the population at the lowest published prices. But it is
the **only** audited, code-level view of the price and mix shift, and it says
plainly where the revenue growth in this market comes from: not from more
patients (enrollees tested fell 15% in six years) and not from routine chemistry
(non-genetic spending is falling), but from a small number of molecular and
genetic codes paid at 42.3x the price of a metabolic panel. That is the
mechanism behind every CAGR in the table above, and it is a mix effect, not a
volume effect.

## What we publish

**Headline: USD 315.14 bn (2026) → USD 459.58 bn (2032) at 6.49%.**

Source: **Mordor Intelligence**, page last updated 31 July 2026 with estimates
stated as of January 2026. Mordor publishes 295.93 bn for 2025, **315.14 bn for
2026** and **431.56 bn for 2031** at a stated 6.49%, and that series is
internally consistent from either starting point — 295.93 to 431.56 over six
years implies **6.4900%**, and 315.14 to 431.56 over five years implies
**6.4897%**. No other house in the table reconciles that cleanly from two
independent starting points.

**Chosen for one decisive reason: it is the only series in the set that states
the services/IVD boundary.** Mordor's published scope is "the revenue earned
from diagnostic testing services performed on patient specimens", explicitly
**excluding sales of instruments, reagents and standalone software** and
research-only testing. Every other house either states no exclusions at all, or
— in 360iResearch's case — segments the market by instrument technology, and in
Data Bridge's case by drug discovery and clinical trial services. The authoring
brief's instruction on this market was to keep "clinical laboratory services"
apart from "diagnostics" and "IVD"; exactly one publisher has done that in
writing, so that is the publisher.

**The 2032 endpoint is interpolated and labelled as such on the page.** Mordor's
own far endpoint is 2031 at 431.56 bn. Advancing that series one further year at
**Mordor's own stated 6.49%** gives the 2032 value of **459.58 bn**:

```
315.14 x 1.0649^6 = 459.58
```

The series therefore passes through the publisher's own 2031 figure as a visible
self-check — the modelled 2031 point is **431.57** against Mordor's published
**431.56**, a 0.01 difference that is the rounding in the published 6.49% and
nothing else. The one-year extension is stated in `forecast_analysis`, in
`data_validation` and in a FAQ. `market_forecast` is seven points at a constant
6.49%: 315.14, 335.59, 357.37, 380.57, 405.26, 431.57, 459.58. The intermediate
years are interpolation, not separately sourced observations, and the chart title
says so.

**The spread is carried onto the page as the finding, not buried.** The 1.480x
base-year spread, the 1.97x CAGR spread, the fact that the CAGR spread is the
wider one, and the reason — that growth here is a forecast of the Protecting
Access to Medicare Act rather than of laboratory volume — are in the executive
summary, the forecast analysis, the key highlights and the FAQs. Readers on a
narrower definition are pointed to the 215.88-242.95 bn cluster for 2026 and to
the 3.5-4.35% rates, and told that those houses state no exclusions either.

Unit: USD Billion. Industry: Life Sciences. Base year 2026, forecast period
2026-2032, historical period 2022-2025.

Everything else published in the JSON is a register figure, a statutory rate, an
OIG audited total or an SEC disclosure, each traceable to a numbered source
below.

## Do not publish

- **No vendor `market_share` anywhere.** This is the most tempting market in the
  corpus for it, because two SEC registrants publish revenue and a third party
  publishes a global total, and the division is trivial to do. It is not done.
  The reason is stated in the prose instead, with both numbers named: Labcorp's
  Diagnostics segment at **$10,876.5 m** and Quest's diagnostic information
  services at **$10,785 m** are each defined by the company, not by the market
  definition being used as a denominator. Labcorp's Dx segment includes
  occupational, wellness and forensic DNA work and is not purely US; Quest's DIS
  figure includes LifeLabs in Canada; and the denominators on offer disagree by
  **1.480x** at the same base year. A share table would therefore have an
  invented numerator over a disputed denominator, and would move by more than
  ten points depending on which house's total was chosen. Mordor's own page says
  the top five companies are "roughly 35-40% of global revenue" and MarkNtel's
  says the top five are "roughly 35% of total revenue" in the US — both are
  bands, from paywalled models, attached to global and US totals that differ by
  an order of magnitude, and neither is published here.
- **`regional_analysis`: empty.** Four houses publish regional dollars or shares,
  and they do not agree. For the *same* year, Fortune Business Insights puts
  North America at **106.15 bn (2025)** and Straits Research at **95.99 bn
  (2025)** — a 1.106x gap — on shares of 36.46% and 41.2% respectively. Across
  the set the North America share runs 36.46%, 36.8%/37.3% (one page, two
  figures), 38%, 38.2%, 39.50%, 41.2%, 41.3% and 44.5%. More decisively, **the
  headline house publishes no regional dollars at all**, only a 41.3% share for
  2025, so a regional table under this headline would have to borrow another
  house's split and apply it to a total it was not built from. Fortune Business
  Insights' US figure is used in grounding (a) and (c) as a *named third-party
  figure under a test*, and is attributed as such in the prose, which is a
  different act from publishing a regional table.
- **`country_analysis`: empty.** Same reason, worse. The country figures that
  exist — FBI's US 103.79 bn, UK 14.59 bn, Germany 18.28 bn, Japan 17.30 bn,
  China 24.71 bn, India 11.65 bn for 2026 — all come from one house and sit
  under a global total 8.9% lower than the one published here. The CLIA register
  is a US-only register and the OIG total is a US program total; neither is a
  country row inside a global model, and presenting them as one would imply a
  consistent global model that does not exist in public.
- **MarkNtel Advisors' 9.89 bn (2025) US series.** Ruled out absolutely in
  grounding (d): Labcorp's Diagnostics segment alone is 1.0997x it and the two
  largest independents together are 2.087x it. Recorded in the table with the
  arithmetic, never averaged in, never cited as a US figure.
- **SNS Insider's 73.06 bn (2025) US figure.** Not ruled out absolutely, but it
  forces the two largest independents to 28.26% of the United States, which
  contradicts every published hospital-laboratory share in the set, and it is
  1.282% of projected 2025 US health spending against the 2.31% measured for
  2013. Recorded as the low bound of the US range, not published.
- **Precision Business Insights' 5.3%.** The same page's endpoints (249.8 bn to
  317.9 bn over seven years) imply about 3.5%, which the page also states. The
  3.5% is recorded; the 5.3% is not used.
- **Data Bridge's 319.13 bn (2024) and 7.5% as a comparable clinical-laboratory
  figure.** Its own application segmentation includes drug discovery, drug
  development, toxicology, cell and gene therapy and preclinical and clinical
  trial services. That is contract research organisation revenue. Recorded as
  the high bound with the scope reason stated; not blended with the others.
- **Any derived United States market size.** The temptation is strong — the CLIA
  register, the OIG total, the PLOS share and the national accounts are all US
  and all public. They are used as *tests*, and grounding (c) deliberately
  presents its result as a fork between two readings rather than resolving it.
  No US total is published as this report's own estimate.
- **Per-laboratory, per-requisition, per-encounter and per-test figures as
  measurements.** $3,374,626 per certified laboratory, $10,246,456 per
  laboratory on a global numerator, $44.20 per requisition, $62.15 per encounter
  and $14.50 per test are plausibility tests computed from published totals over
  published denominators. Three of the five are deliberately mismatched ratios
  used as bounds. `data_validation` says so, and every appearance on the page is
  labelled.
- **The 70% claim** — that 70% of medical decisions depend on laboratory
  results. It appears throughout this sector's marketing. The literature on it
  is explicit that it traces to a 2005 Lewin Group report without citable
  references and has "apparently gained legitimacy simply due to the number of
  times that it had been repeated" (source 17). Dropped entirely. The 2.3%
  spending share from the same body of literature *is* used, because it was
  independently validated against the national accounts in grounding (c).
- **A single "the clinical laboratory services market is X" headline without the
  spread.** Dropped by construction: the title, meta description, summary,
  executive summary and first FAQ all carry the 1.480x and 1.97x spreads and the
  scope caveat before or alongside the number.
- **Segment percentages.** Clinical chemistry is given as 56.60% (Mordor, 2025),
  58.0% (Coherent, 2026), 31.35% (Data Bridge, 2025) and 42.4% (GMI, 2022). Four
  houses, four answers, no agreement on what clinical chemistry is.
  `market_segmentation` is published as plain strings with no shares attached.

# Digital pathology — research notes
**Slug:** digital-pathology-market   **Drafted:** 2026-10-18

Digital pathology is bounded by two things that can be counted exactly. The
first is the number of whole-slide imaging systems the FDA has authorised for
use on surgical pathology slides — a single product code, `PSY`, under one
regulation, 21 CFR 864.3700, with one de novo and a short list of 510(k)s after
it. The second is the number of laboratories that could install one, which CMS
itself counts and, in an October 2026 proposed rule, itself prices. So the
usual impossible question — does a published CAGR describe anything physical? —
is here answerable by division. This file does it four ways. The answer is that
no published growth rate in the literature funds the installed base it is sold
as a forecast of, and that the single reimbursement mechanism cited everywhere
as the adoption driver has never once been mentioned in the Federal Register.

## The spread

**Headline, across every scope on a 2026 footing: $714M to $11,818M — a 16.55x
spread.** As with most of this literature that number is an artefact, but the
artefact here is *not* mainly a software-versus-hardware tier difference. Three
distinct things are being sold as "the digital pathology market":

| Tier | 2026 value | What it counts |
|---|---|---|
| Computational / AI pathology only | $715M - $1,871M | Algorithms that read the image. Five publishers, 2.62x apart among themselves |
| **The digitisation stack** | **$714M - $2,010M** | Scanners + viewing/management software + storage. Eleven publishers |
| The stack plus deployment services | $11,818M | FMI, which adds installation, workflow configuration, validation support and maintenance — and is 5.88x the next highest estimate |

The computational-pathology tier **overlaps** the stack tier numerically rather
than sitting below it: Grand View publishes $1,600M for digital pathology and
$781.5M for computational pathology, so on its own figures the algorithms are
48.8% of the whole stack, while Verified Market Research's *entire* digital
pathology market, $714M, is **below** three of the five computational-pathology
estimates. That is not a tier structure. It is a definitional collision, and a
reader cannot tell from any headline which of the two markets they have bought.

**Inside the eleven-estimate digitisation-stack tier:**

```
2026 values, sorted:  714.02  878.47  1390  1418.75  1470  1600  1660  1680  1793.3  1886.17  2010
low to high:          $2,010M / $714.02M = 2.815x       median $1,600M

CAGRs, sorted:        7.99  8.6  9.90  10.19  12.7  13.5  13.6  13.8  16.05  18.3  18.56
low to high:          18.56% / 7.99% = 2.323x           median 13.5%

ratio of spreads:     2.815 / 2.323 = 1.212
```

**This is the finding, and it is the opposite of a clean disagreement.** In a
market where publishers agree on the thing and argue about growth, the size
spread is narrow and the CAGR spread is wide; in a market where they are
counting different things, the reverse. Digital pathology is **neither**: size
and growth disagree by almost exactly the same multiple, 2.82x against 2.32x,
a ratio of 1.21. There is no dimension along which the literature converges.
Carried to 2032 on each publisher's own rate the two disagreements compound
rather than cancel:


and a growth rate from another is not interpolating — they are combining two
estimates that disagree about which end of the market is large.

## Grounding arithmetic

### 1. The headline division — dollars per laboratory that could install one

```
$2,010M (Mordor, 2026) / 33,955 CoC+CoA laboratories          = $59,196 per non-waived lab
$2,010M               / 13,582 CMS "digital-capable" CoC/CoA  = $147,990 per lab
$2,010M               /  3,229 cytology-subspecialty labs     = $622,484 per lab
```

**The entire global digital pathology market, allocated across the 13,582 US
laboratories CMS itself nominates as digitally capable, is $147,990 per
laboratory per year — 1.48 times the bottom of CMS's own $100,000-$1,500,000
range for one scanner.** And that is the generous reading: it gives the whole
world's spending to one country's laboratories. Apply the publishers' own North
America shares and it falls to $59,196-$70,739 per lab.

### 2. What each candidate CAGR implies per laboratory by 2032

Hold the denominator at 13,582 — justified, because the non-waived count has
not moved in five years [11][12][18]:

```
                 2032 global market      per digital-capable lab
```

**The entire 2.32x disagreement about growth moves the per-laboratory figure
from $234,711 to $411,019 — and every one of those numbers sits in the lower
third of CMS's own one-scanner cost range.** The distance between the most
pessimistic and the most aggressive published forecast of this market is, per
candidate laboratory, $176,308 a year: about one mid-range scanner. Nothing in
the published CAGR spread is a claim about whether the installed base gets
built. All of them are claims about how slowly it does not.

### 3. The decisive division — cumulative spend against the cost of the base

This is the test the register makes possible and nobody runs. Sum the whole
published forecast, 2026 through 2032, and compare it with what CMS says it
costs to equip the laboratories:

```
Cumulative global market 2026-2032 at 9.90%:
  2010.00 + 2208.99 + 2427.68 + 2668.02 + 2932.15 + 3222.44 + 3541.46 = $19,010.7M
Cumulative at 15.00%:                                                   $22,244.3M

Cost of equipping the 13,582 CMS-identified digital-capable laboratories,
at CMS's own stated range:
  13,582 x   $100,000 = $1,358.2M     (low end)
  13,582 x $1,500,000 = $20,373.0M    (high end)
```

Three ratios, all printed:

```
$1,358.2M  / $2,010M     = 67.6%   — equipping every candidate US lab at CMS's
                                      CHEAPEST price is two-thirds of one year of
                                      the entire GLOBAL market
$20,373.0M / $19,010.7M  = 1.072x  — equipping them at CMS's HIGH price exceeds
                                      the entire cumulative global market
                                      2026-2032 at the rate we publish
$20,373.0M / $22,244.3M  = 91.6%   — and still consumes 92% of it at 15%
```

Now take the United States out of the global total using the publishers' own
regional shares — North America 47.8% (Mordor, 2025) and 39.92% (Grand View,
2025) [23][24]:

```
$19,010.7M x 0.478   = $9,087.1M ;  / 13,582 = $669,057 per lab, cumulative over 7 years
$19,010.7M x 0.3992  = $7,589.1M ;  / 13,582 = $558,761 per lab, cumulative over 7 years
At 15%: $22,244.3M x 0.478 = $10,632.8M ; / 13,582 = $782,857 per lab
```

**Seven years of North American digital pathology spending, every dollar of it
— scanners, software, storage, service, AI — comes to $558,761 to $782,857 per
candidate laboratory. CMS's own midpoint for one scanner is $800,000.** The
published forecasts do not fund a complete US installed base by 2032 at any
rate in the literature, and the gap is not marginal: it is between 1.0x and
2.7x short on the equipment line alone, before anyone is paid to run it.

**What this rules in.** Digital pathology through 2032 is a **partial-adoption**
market: large academic and reference laboratories, and the dermatopathology
and independent-laboratory segments that already run at volume. The 8-19%
published CAGRs are consistent with an installed base that reaches a minority
of candidate laboratories, and are not consistent with the "digital transition
of pathology" framing that accompanies them. **What it rules out.** Any reading
in which the published market size and the published CAGR together describe
general adoption. They cannot: the arithmetic above is the market's own numbers
against the regulator's own costs.

### 4. One scanner authorisation per 590 candidate laboratories

```
13,582 CMS digital-capable CoC/CoA laboratories / 23 PSY authorisations = 590.5
13,582 / 10 distinct PSY applicants                                     = 1,358.2
33,955 non-waived laboratories / 23                                     = 1,476.3
```

**There are 590 candidate laboratories for every whole-slide imaging
authorisation the FDA has ever granted, and 1,358 for every company that holds
one.** Eight of the 23 belong to a single applicant. This is the structural
fact the market estimates obscure: supply-side concentration is extreme, the
authorised universe is 46 devices in total, and three of the ten PSY applicants
(Philips, Leica Biosystems, Roche's Ventana) are divisions of companies whose
own disclosures are examined below.

### 5. Containment — the market against one disclosed pathology line

revenue line at all. Roche reports **Pathology Lab** as a Diagnostics Division

Pathology Lab was the division's fastest-growing customer area in 2025. At the
IRS yearly average rate of **CHF 0.831 per US dollar for 2025** [19]:

```
CHF 1,723M / 0.831 = $2,073.4M
$2,073.4M / $2,010M (Mordor, 2026, global digital pathology) = 1.032x
$2,073.4M / $1,390M (Precedence, 2026)                       = 1.492x
$2,073.4M / $714.0M (Verified Market Research, 2026)         = 2.904x
CHF 1,599M / 0.881 (2024 rate) = $1,815.0M for the prior year
```

**One company's anatomic-pathology customer area is larger than the entire
global digital pathology market as nine of the eleven publishers size it.**
That does not mean the publishers are wrong. It means the thing they are sizing
is a thin digitisation layer sitting on top of a much larger tissue-diagnostics
business — stainers, antibodies, probes, consumables, service — of which Roche
alone books $2.07bn. The register agrees: Roche holds 2 of the 23 `PSY`
authorisations and is not the largest holder. **Anyone who reads a digital

**Not one Federal Register document in the history of the publication contains

national payment amount, never discussed them in a fee schedule rule, and never
mentioned digital pathology in the CY2023 or CY2026 Physician Fee Schedule or
the CY2026 Outpatient PPS final rules at all.** Category III codes carry no
relative value units; whatever is paid is paid at a Medicare Administrative
Contractor's discretion, case by case, with no published rate.

conversion factor is $33.4009** for non-qualifying APM and **$33.5675** for
qualifying APM, against $32.3465 in CY2025 [13]. 88305 itself is paid under the
Physician Fee Schedule with a professional/technical split, not under the
Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule — the CLFS prices clinical laboratory tests,
not physician interpretation of tissue.

**The widely-cited reimbursement driver for this market is a set of thirteen
codes with no assigned payment amount, no mention in any fee schedule rule, and
an explicit prohibition on reporting them for the AI training and validation
use case that is the fastest-growing part of the market.** That is the single
most load-bearing finding in this file, and it is established by absence in a
searchable primary register rather than by anyone's assertion.

### 7. The CLIA rule that is actually moving, and what it costs

The October 2026 proposed rule is not about reimbursement; it is about whether
a pathologist may review a digital image away from the testing site at all. CMS
proposes to add "digital images, digital data" to the records laboratories must
store under 42 CFR 493.1101(e), to revise the retention rules at 493.1105 —
cytology slide preparations retained at least 5 years, histopathology slides at
least 10 years — and to permit "virtual access", defined to include "the test
analysis, transmission of results, images, or data via a secure connection,
review at a secure location, and transmission back to the primary testing
laboratory" [11]. It also proposes to allow digital images in gynecologic
cytology proficiency testing "to align with the Food and Drug
Administration (FDA)-authorized digital cytology screening systems", and
estimates that 3,229 cytology-subspecialty laboratories are affected, of which
it assumes "approximately 25 percent... (807 cytology laboratories) have
adopted digital images for screening as their primary method of patient
testing" [11].

```
807 / 3,229 = 25.0%  — CMS's own assumed digital adoption rate in cytology,
                       and it says plainly: "We are unable to determine how many
                       laboratories have adopted digital images for screening as
                       the primary method of patient testing."
```

**The regulator does not know the installed base either, and says so in print.**
Its 25% assumption is the only published adoption figure traceable to a
government source, and against 13,582 candidate laboratories it would imply
roughly 3,400 digitised laboratories nationally — consistent with the
partial-adoption reading in division 3 and inconsistent with any reading in
which US pathology is mid-transition.
